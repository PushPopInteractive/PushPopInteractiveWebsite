How to Play

The Returner's Handbook

You arrive, you register, and then the world is open. This is what everything on the form means and what to do once you have handed it back.

Arriving

You begin at a counter in the main branch of the Adventurer's Guild, in the human capital of Eldrath. Every Returner in the world begins here — there is no other door in.

Lilia recognises what you are immediately, because she has been doing this all week, and hands you a form.

  1. Appearance and nameChoose how you look and what to call yourself. Names are unique across the entire world — there is only one of you.
  2. Spend forty pointsYour four scores each start at 10. You have forty points to place on top of that, however you like.
  3. Choose a classOne of eight. This is the single most consequential line on the form.
  4. Spend one more pointEarning your class title comes with a point. Put it wherever you want.

Then you walk through into the real guild hall, and you are in the world. The form recorded who you are and what you intend to do for a living. It did not build your body — you arrived in that already.

The three vessels

Every Returner comes through in one of three bodies. This is not a fantasy race; it is what your soul landed in. Alrathel has sixteen peoples of its own, and you are not one of them.

Summon

Your own living human body, crossed over intact.

Passive

The same harmful condition applied to you repeatedly within a minute wears off noticeably faster each time.

Activated

Restore a quarter of your endurance at once, and a tenth of your health over ten seconds.

Drawback

Ordinary human sight. No low-light vision at all — you are the only vessel that genuinely cannot see in the dark.

Amalgam

An undead frame assembled from human parts.

Passive

Immune to disease. Bleeding and poison last half as long on you.

Activated

Out of combat, channel for six seconds to restore a quarter of your health. Twenty-minute cooldown.

Drawback

Your maximum mana is a tenth lower than it would otherwise be.

Construct

A humanoid golem of bronze and gold, with exotic eyes.

Passive

Physical damage reduced slightly. Ordinary bleeding and biological disease simply do not apply to you.

Activated

Cut incoming physical damage by a fifth and ignore forced movement for eight seconds — at half speed.

Drawback

Sprinting costs you noticeably more endurance than it costs anyone else.

Activated vessel abilities run on a fifteen-minute cooldown unless stated otherwise. Cooldowns survive death, changing regions, logging out and coming back tomorrow.

The four scores

Returnity's scores are B.A.R.D.: Buffness, Agility, Resonance, Durability. They are never rolled. Each begins at 10 — the ordinary-adult baseline, not a lucky number — and you place forty points on top at creation.

Each score does exactly one job in a fight, plus qualifying you for equipment. None of them quietly improves something unrelated.

ScoreIts one job
BuffnessMultiplies physical weapon damage. Every point above 10 adds one per cent — so the same score is worth more absolute damage on a better weapon.
AgilityWhether ordinary physical attacks connect. Each point of difference between attacker and defender is one full percentage point, in either direction.
ResonanceMagical and spiritual conductivity. It is not intelligence and it does not make you clever.
DurabilityHow much you can take.

There is no upper cap you can see, no 6-to-18 range, and no conversion table. Every point counts directly, at full value, forever.

You finish creation with 41 earned points. Every level after that grants one more, freely assigned, to a total of 90 at level 50. A very few rare accomplishments out in the world grant permanent extra points; nobody will tell you which ones.

Classes grant mechanics, never numbers

Your class does not set your scores, your health, or what you are allowed to wear. Armour and weapons state their own score requirements — so any class can use any item, provided the character actually qualifies for it. A wizard in plate is a question of Buffness and Durability, not of permission.

The eight classes

Eight classes in four archetypes. You pick the class, not the archetype.

Knight — Fighter

Guarding and tanking. Techniques built for a two-handed weapon, or for a one-hander and a shield — both styles are fully supported, not one real option and one courtesy.

Berzerker — Fighter

Aggressive weapon damage, dual wielding included. The archetype's answer to a problem is more of the problem, faster.

Wizard — Mage

Elemental damage, utility magic, and teleportation. Entirely distinct from the Necromancer — they do not overlap and are not meant to.

Necromancer — Mage

Dead minions, golems, life drain and poison. The only class whose staff is also a staffing arrangement.

Cleric — Priest

Holy healing, the destruction of undead, and resurrection. The person your party will be very glad to have when things stop going well.

Druid — Priest

Nature magic, healing over time, roots and snares. Keeps everyone alive by keeping the enemy exactly where it was.

Ranger — Rogue

Bows, dual-wielded one-handers, tracking, and prepared traps — battlefield objects you set before the fight and fight around.

Rogue — Rogue

Stealth, backstabbing, dual-wielded one-handers, utility, and bombs — devices used in the moment rather than laid in advance.

Every class keeps a believable way to work alone against ordinary enemies at its own level. Grouping is safer and faster; it is not the only way to make progress.

Abilities and spells

Your class abilities arrive on their own. There is no trainer to visit, nothing to purchase, and no skill points to spend — an ability family unlocks the moment you reach its level, and later ranks quietly replace earlier ones as you keep growing.

Knowing a spell is not the same as having it ready. You carry eight prepared slots, and choosing what goes in them is the real decision. That choice is yours, every time.

Mythic Rites

Separate from all of the above, every class has exactly one Mythic Rite: a world-scale capstone with a mechanic that belongs to it alone. Rites are not learned by levelling. They are found — in endgame quests, in what large things are guarding, out in the world.

Levelling, and after

Returners advance to level 50. Death never costs you experience — not a point, not ever.

Once you reach 50, further experience becomes Alternate Advancement. It is never banked toward some future level increase; the cap is the cap. AA is durable, finite progress rather than an endless ladder, and the level bar fills first — if a single reward crosses the cap, only the genuine overflow goes into AA.

Fighting

Combat is deliberate rather than frantic. You select a target, start or stop your automatic attacks, and use abilities on top of them. Sitting recovers health, mana and endurance faster — and is, on many days, the single most useful thing you can do.

Whether you are in combat matters, because several abilities refuse to work during it. A fight is over ten seconds after the last hostile action in either direction.

You carry both a melee and a ranged weapon set and switch between them.

Light is scenery, not a rule

Plenty of things in Alrathel care about daylight, darkness, temperature, terrain and hazards — vessel drawbacks, Nocturne affinities, dark elf accuracy in the sun. None of it is judged by what your screen happens to be rendering. The world itself decides where you are standing and what conditions apply there, consistently, for everyone.

What your class notices

Every class carries the same map. What differs is the private layer drawn on top of it: within about eighty metres, your training shows you things nobody else can see.

ClassWhat you notice
KnightHostile creatures and people.
BerzerkerAnyone currently in a fight.
WizardExposed magical items, and magical traps.
NecromancerCorpses, and the undead.
ClericDemons, and the undead.
DruidNatural animals.
RangerEverything living nearby, coloured by whether it likes you.
RogueTraps, mundane and magical alike.

These are dots. They give you a position and nothing else — no name, no level, no contents, no amount, no species, no idea what the trap does. Zooming in does not extend the range, and nobody in your party sees your layer unless you tell them what is on it.

Companions

Adventure pets are cosmetic in the sense that they never fight. They cannot be attacked, cannot be targeted, take no damage, draw nothing's attention, trip no traps, and leave no corpse. What they do is notice things.

You may take exactly one on an adventure. It can walk with you or ride stowed in your pack — stowing hides it and costs a storage slot but does not switch off what it does. Its senses stack with your class's, though the ranges never add together.

Because one pet occupies your only pet slot for the entire outing, the bonus has to be worth taking instead of every other pet. They come in three grades:

GradeShape of the bonusExamples
CommonModest, broadly useful on any ordinary day. Bought from Nelly Saye in the capital market.Weasel (traps nearby) · Cat (animals) · Parrot (gold on corpses and in unopened chests) · Rabbit (hostiles) · Pocket Tortoise (faster sitting recovery) · Sugar Glider (softer landings)
UncommonStronger, but tied to a recognisable activity or environment. Given by someone, after you have done something for them.Owl (magic items and wards) · Raven (corpses) · White Dove (demons and undead) · Arctic Lemming (cold) · Pygmy River Otter (swimming and breath)
RareVery powerful in a deliberately narrow set of circumstances. Several are not given at all.Cave Bat (hidden passages) · Pearl Crab (submerged caches) · Lantern Wisp (things stealthed nearby) · Pebble Golem (unstable stone) · Clockwork Mouse (working machinery)

The uncommon and rare companions are earned through commissions or acquired by less official means. A cave bat, for instance, is currently in a locked cellar belonging to somebody in the capital's black market who would rather keep it.

Dying

Death in Returnity costs you time and inconvenience. It does not cost you experience and it never costs you a character.

  • You leave a real corpse where you fell, holding your equipment, your inventory and the coin you were carrying. It stands there for 48 real-world hours.
  • Nobody can loot it but you. You may give another character permission to drag your corpse. That is never permission to open it.
  • You come back at Lilia's counter in the Adventurer's Guild, whole, with your vitals restored — and with none of your things. Those are still lying where you left them.
  • Dragging is slow. A helper takes hold at close range and tows the corpse behind them, slowed according to its mass and how strong they are. No sprinting while dragging.
  • Resurrection needs a living caster, the correct un-expired corpse, and proximity to it. That is what a Cleric is for.

The Adventurer's Guild

The guild hall in Eldrath is the hub of early adventuring life and the main place work comes from. It holds a public quest board that continually refills with commissions — when a slot is claimed and closed, the board posts something new in its place. The board is shared with everybody in the world, so what is on it depends partly on who got there first.

Hesta Dain is the board clerk. She explains commissions, handles claims, and reads your rank off your level. There is no exam.

RankLevelsWhat it means
F1–9New. Everybody starts here.
E10–19Working.
D20–29Established.
C30–39Serious.
B40–49Formidable.
A50–59The top of what a Returner reaches. Balanced for a full party of level 50s.
S60+Multi-party epic raids. Nothing in the world goes past this.

Your rank is a label for your level and nothing more. There is no promotion, no demotion, and no separate guild-rank progress to grind.

Playing together

A party holds six. A Living Adventurer takes one of those six places, exactly like a person would. The rare story figure who joins you for a job is a temporary seventh and does not need a place to be free.

Chat

  • /say — the people standing near you.
  • /zone and /ooc — everyone in your region.
  • /group — your party, wherever in the world they are.
  • /tell — one person, directly, across any distance.

Player guilds have their own channel. Blocks and mutes apply consistently everywhere — tells, invitations, friend requests, targeted emotes — and refusals always read the same way, so nobody can work out from a message whether they have been blocked, muted, or simply missed.

Getting about

Your map fills in only where you personally go, or where someone reliable tells you what is there. Nothing is shared with your other characters or the party beside you. Full detail on the World page, but the short version:

  • Wagons run the settled overland routes. You can only buy passage to somewhere you already know, and they stop running after dark on any road that is not settled heartland.
  • Tunnel Runners carry passengers along the real passages of the Underworld, between The Light Below, Deepforge and Val'thar.
  • Swimming is genuinely dangerous. Breath drains while your head is under, and running out of it kills you.

Money and property

Banking

Returners bank in the four human cities — Eldrath, Tidehaven, Winterhold and Kaldron. Banks elsewhere in the world are perfectly real institutions that simply do not keep Returner accounts, and will politely decline you.

An account belongs to one character, never to a player. Deposits and withdrawals carry no fee. Banked items weigh nothing, cannot be equipped where they sit, and never appear on a corpse.

The post

Mail is how one Returner reaches another when they are never online at the same time. A letter is addressed to a character by name — names being unique, that is the whole address — and carries text, coin, and up to four items. The sender pays; the recipient owes nothing.

Letters take real time to travel, by the shortest route between the counter you posted at and the counter you addressed. Once it arrives you can read it anywhere. The coin and the parcel are handed over at the destination counter, in person.

The auction house

It stands in the guild district's civic plaza, beside the bank and the post office. Sellers set a starting bid and may set a buyout; buyers bid it up or pay the buyout and end it immediately. Listings run for a set time and close.

Everything is collected at the counter — won items, sale proceeds, refunds on being outbid, withdrawn listings, unsold goods. Nothing is ever pushed into an absent player's pack or purse.

Property

Housing in the cities carries a monthly tax, drawn from banked gold. Corpse recovery fees are taken the same way, from banked coin before carried coin.

Harvesting and making things

Crafting is not a skill you level. It is a person you have to find.

You harvest raw materials out in the world — thirty-seven of them, from three dozen kinds of node, scattered across every region. That is your half of the work, and it is the reason to go somewhere.

Then you find the crafter. There are sixteen of them: a retired blacksmith, an alchemist hermit, a glassmaker, a reedwright. They know the recipes; you never will. And they will turn you down, because they do not know you — so you do them a favour first, and it is a real favour, out in the world.

Consequences worth understanding before you go looking:

  • There is no crafting level, no crafting bar, no station and no skill-ups.
  • A recipe belongs to a specific person. Two crafters may refine the same ore into two different things.
  • The gate is social and geographic — find them, earn them — never a number you grind past.
  • The material is the ticket. The crafter is the point.

Cooking, fishing and pastimes

Cooking

Every Returner can cook, and it is never gated by class. There is no cooking level, no experience bar, no success roll, no failure ladder, and no critical result. What progresses is knowledge — recipes, methods, and understanding your ingredients. Your scores have nothing to do with the quality of a meal, though they have a great deal to do with obtaining the ingredients.

Fishing

Fishing is neither crafting nor harvesting. Water is never claimed, never depleted, never contested — two people can fish the same bank at the same moment and neither is in the other's way.

The mechanic is timing, not tables. You cast, the water takes its own time to give you a bite, and you get a short window to strike. Reel early and the hook comes up empty. Reel late and it took the bait and left.

The Arena

Consensual one-on-one exhibitions between Returners, or selectable enemy groups fought for rewards. You register solo — which admits only you, even if your whole party is standing there — or as a party, which admits the leader and every eligible member physically present at the counter, up to six. Only the leader can start the party branch.

Breakers

The tavern dice game. Two seats, one pot, and the sea keeps what you do not bank.

  • Both players ante 5 copper. The winner takes the pot of 10.
  • On your turn you roll two dice as often as you dare. Every roll adds its sum to your turn total.
  • A seven breaks the wave: your turn total is wiped and the dice pass.
  • Bank at any time to lock the turn total onto your score and pass the dice.
  • First to 50 banked wins immediately.
  • Leaving the table — forfeiting, dying, changing region, disconnecting, or closing the window mid-game — hands the pot to your opponent.

Doubles are nothing special. One clean rule, no exceptions.

Rules of the world

  • One world. Returnity is a single shared world, not a collection of separate copies with different names.
  • Day lasts two real hours, and so does night. Plan around it, particularly if you are Nocturne, Dhampir, a dark elf, or trying to catch the last wagon.
  • Your map is your own. So is your bank account, your mail, and your fog of war. Nothing is inherited between your characters.
  • Death is expensive in time, never in progress.
